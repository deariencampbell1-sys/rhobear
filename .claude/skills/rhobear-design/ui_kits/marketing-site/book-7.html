LESSON 7.1

Four verbs make a model.

"Everyone says 'training' like it's one thing. It's four different jobs, stacked. Learn the ladder and the whole field stops being fog."

There is a ladder every serious model climbs, and each rung is a different verb with different data, different cost, and a different thing being changed. Mix them up and nothing anyone says about AI will make sense. Here they are, bottom to top: pretrain, fine-tune, align, distill — and then a fifth, quantize, which Book 6 already taught. ◇ Checkpoint 7.1
"Training from scratch" means…
Pretraining. From random numbers to a model that knows how language works, by reading a huge pile of text. It is the bottom rung and by far the priciest — everything above it (fine-tune, align, distill) is cheap polish on top of that one enormous investment.
LESSON 7.2

A model never sees a letter.

"Before any learning happens, the text has to become numbers. The rulebook that does it — the tokenizer — quietly decides how smart and how cheap your model can be."

A model reads tokens, not words — chunks of text mapped to numbers. The tokenizer is trained once, before the model, using byte-level BPE (Byte-Pair Encoding): start from raw bytes so nothing is ever "unknown," then repeatedly merge the most common pairs into single tokens. Common words become one token; rare ones split into pieces.

The vocabulary size is a real design lever. DeepSeek's 67B model used a 100K-token vocabulary; DeepSeek-V3 widened it to 128K, with the pre-tokenizer tuned for multilingual compression. Bigger vocab = fewer tokens per sentence = cheaper and faster — but a fatter embedding table. Type below and watch text turn into tokens.

◇ Checkpoint 7.2
Why start the tokenizer from raw bytes?
No unknowns, ever. A word-level tokenizer chokes on anything it never saw. Byte-level BPE can always fall back to raw bytes, so a brand-new name, a foreign script, or a weird symbol is spelled out in pieces instead of becoming a blank. That robustness is why every serious model uses it.
LESSON 7.3

Guess the next word. A trillion times.

"The whole miracle is one boring game played at unimaginable scale: cover the next token, guess it, get graded, nudge the weights. Repeat until it has read the internet."

Pretraining is one objective: next-token prediction. Show the model some text, hide the next token, let it output a probability for every token in the vocabulary, and score it with cross-entropy loss — high when it's surprised by the truth, low when it saw it coming. To learn grammar, facts, code, and reasoning, it must get good at this one game. DeepSeek-V3 played it across 14.8 trillion tokens.

The thing that turns "you were wrong" into a slightly better model is the optimizer. The standard is AdamW — it keeps a running memory of each weight's gradient direction and size, so it takes confident steps on stable signals and cautious ones on noisy weights, with weight decay pulling weights gently toward zero to avoid overfitting. The learning rate isn't constant: it warms up from near zero (so early chaos doesn't wreck the model), holds, then decays down so the final steps are delicate. Move the cursor to watch the schedule.

◇ Checkpoint 7.3
Why does next-token prediction teach so much — grammar, facts, even reasoning?
Prediction forces understanding. To guess the token after "the capital of France is" you must know a fact; after a math setup, you must follow logic. One dumb game, played over trillions of tokens, squeezes real competence out of the model — no human labels required. That is why it's called self-supervised learning.
LESSON 7.4

671 billion, but only 37 fire.

"The trick that lets a model be huge and affordable at the same time: build a giant, but only ever wake a small piece of it for each word."

Here is the p-word the whole field turns on: parameters — the model's knobs. A normal ("dense") model runs every parameter for every token, so bigger always means slower and pricier. A Mixture of Experts breaks that link. It splits the network into many experts and adds a router that, for each token, picks only a few to run.

So a model can hold 671B total parameters of knowledge but only activate ~37B per token — the smarts of a giant at the running cost of a mid-size model. The number that decides your compute bill is the active count, not the total. Drag the router.

LESSON 7.5

Many small specialists, plus a few generalists.

"Ordinary Mixture-of-Experts wastes its experts. DeepSeek's twist was to make each one sharper — and to keep a couple of experts always on for the boring shared stuff."

DeepSeekMoE adds two ideas that squeeze real specialization out of the experts. First, fine-grained segmentation: instead of a few fat experts, split each into many slim ones (by slicing the FFN's hidden dimension), so knowledge can be carved into finer pieces and combined more flexibly — at the same compute cost. Second, shared expert isolation: reserve a couple of experts that every token always uses, to soak up common knowledge, so the routed specialists don't each waste capacity re-learning the basics.

◇ Checkpoint 7.5
What is the job of a shared expert?
It carries the common load. Without a shared expert, every routed specialist would independently re-learn the boring shared stuff — wasted, redundant capacity. Isolate that into always-on experts and each specialist is freed to get truly good at its niche. Cleaner division of labor, more specialization per parameter.
LESSON 7.6

Stop every token piling onto one expert.

"Left alone, the router gets lazy — it sends everything to a few favorite experts and lets the rest rot. Fixing that without hurting the model was a genuinely new trick."

Routers have a failure mode called routing collapse: a few experts get picked constantly, the rest sit idle and never learn, and your giant MoE quietly behaves like a small dense model. The old fix was an auxiliary loss — a penalty that punishes imbalance. It works, but it fights the main objective and slightly dents quality, because you're forcing balance the model didn't want.

DeepSeek-V3's answer was auxiliary-loss-free load balancing: give each expert a small learnable bias added to its routing score only for the pick — nudge overloaded experts down and starved ones up — but keep that bias out of the actual weighting. Balance happens as a gentle steering, with no penalty term dragging on quality. Toggle it.

Why is a learnable bias better than an auxiliary loss for balancing experts?
Balance without a tax. An auxiliary loss is a second, competing objective — it pulls the model away from "predict well" toward "be balanced," and quality pays for it. The bias trick achieves the same balance as a gentle nudge on which expert gets picked, while leaving the loss — and the quality — alone.

◇ Checkpoint 7.7
Multi-head Latent Attention (MLA) mainly reduces…
It shrinks the KV cache. During generation, a model normally stores a key and value for every past token — memory that balloons on long chats. MLA compresses those into a small latent vector, slashing the memory cost of long context. That's what makes 128K-token conversations affordable to serve.
LESSON 7.8

Fine-tuning vs distilling.

"You asked me the difference straight up. Here it is, side by side — because people use the words interchangeably and they are not the same move."

Both take a pretrained model and make it better at something specific. The difference is where the lessons come from. In plain fine-tuning (SFT) you feed a model curated example pairs — prompt in, ideal answer out — and it learns to imitate them. In distillation, a bigger, smarter teacher model generates the answers, and a smaller student learns to copy the teacher — you're pouring a giant's skill into a small vessel.

DeepSeek did exactly this: they took the reasoning of their giant R1 model, had it produce ~800,000 worked examples, and fine-tuned small off-the-shelf models (Qwen and Llama) on them. The result — R1-Distill-Qwen-32B — reasons close to models many times its size. Distillation is fine-tuning where the teacher is another AI. That is the exact move Book 6 used to build Forge.

LESSON 7.9

Reasoning nobody taught it.

"This is the one that shook the field. No worked examples, no human reasoning traces — just a reward for being right, and the model grew a mind that thinks."

The last rung is reinforcement learning: instead of copying answers, the model tries, gets a reward, and shifts toward whatever earned it. DeepSeek-R1-Zero did something startling — it took the pretrained base and ran pure RL, with only rule-based rewards: a point for a correct final answer (checked by a verifier or a compiler), and a point for putting its thinking between <think> tags. No human reasoning examples at all.

And reasoning emerged on its own — self-checking, backtracking, spending more time on hard problems. The paper records the model literally pausing mid-solution: "Wait, wait. Wait. That's an aha moment I can flag here. Let's reevaluate this step-by-step." AIME math score jumped from 15.6% to 71.0% (86.7% with voting). Nobody wrote that behavior — the right incentive grew it.

DeepSeek-R1 · arXiv:2501.12948 — GRPO, rule-based rewards, the "aha moment"

The optimizer that made it cheap is GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization), from DeepSeekMath. Normal RL (PPO) needs a second "critic" network to judge each answer — expensive. GRPO throws the critic away: sample a group of answers to the same question, and score each one relative to its group's average. Above average → push the model toward it; below → away. The advantage is just (reward − group mean) ÷ group spread. Roll a fresh group:

LESSON 7.10

R1 to R4? Not quite.

"You asked how they went from R1 to R4. Here's the real answer — and it's a better story than a version counter."

There is no public R2, R3, or R4. The reasoning line (R1) didn't march forward as its own product — it merged back into the flagship line. The lesson worth keeping: capabilities in this field converge. "Reasoning" stopped being a separate model and became a mode inside the main one. Here is the actual lineage.
